Suppose we have a portfolio ${p}$ consiting of ${2}$ assets ${A}$ and ${B}$, with respective weights ${w_A}$ and ${w_B}$.
The Variance of the portfolio ${p}$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \Var(p) = \Var(w_A A + w_B B) = \Cov(w_A A + w_B B, w_A A + w_B B)
\end{equation} 

Thanks to the bilinearity of covariance, we can expand the above equation as follows :

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Var(p) &= w_A^2 \Var(A) + w_B^2 \Var(B) + 2 w_A w_B \Cov(A, B) \\ 
        &= w_A^2 \sigma^2_A + w_B^2 \sigma^2_B + 2 w_A w_B \sigma_A \sigma_B \rho_{AB} \text{ , where } \rho_{AB} \text{ is the correlation coefficient.}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Thanks to this equation, we can see that indeed 2 assets with negative correlation would reduce the variance and therefore, the volatility squared of our portfolio.

\vspace{1em}

Now, let's consider the case of ${n}$ assets, with respective weights ${\bw = (w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n)}$ and returns ${r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n}$. We thus have the return of the portfolio ${r_p = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i r_i}$. Treating ${r_i}$ as random variables, we can deduce the following:
\begin{equation}
    \Var(r_p) = \Cov(r_p, r_p) = \Cov\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i r_i, \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j r_j\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_i w_j \Cov(r_i, r_j)
\end{equation}

On the other hand, let $\bSigma$ be a covariance matrix of the returns of the assets, with $\Sigma_{ij} = \Cov(r_i, r_j)$. We therefore have $(\bSigma \bw)_i = \mathlarger{\sum_{j=1}^n \Sigma_{ij} w_j}$ and consequently 
\begin{equation}
\bw^\top \bSigma \bw = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i (\bSigma \bw)_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \sum_{j=1}^{n} \Sigma_{ij} w_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_i w_j \Cov(r_i, r_j) = \Var(r_p)
\end{equation}

We can infer directly that reducing the variance of the portfolio, and therefore its volatily, is equivalent to minimizing the quadraitc form $\bw^\top \bSigma \bw$ with respect to the weights $\bw$.
Assuming we are working with a budget of 1 with a target return of $r_p$, we can formulate the following optimisation problem:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \min_{\bw} \quad & \bw^\top \bSigma \bw \\
        \text{s.t.} \quad & \bw^\top \bmu = r_p \\
        & \bw^\top \bone = 1
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

We solve this optimization problem via Lagrange multipliers, thus, introducing one multiplier per constraint, our Lagrangian is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        &\mathcal{L}(\bw, \lambda, \gamma) = \frac{1}{2} \bw^\top \bSigma \bw - \lambda (\bw^\top \bmu - r_p) - \gamma (\bw^\top \bone - 1)\\
        \Rightarrow & \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \bw} = \bSigma \bw - \lambda \bmu - \gamma \bone \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Setting this partial derivative to zero, we obtain $\bw$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \bw =\bSigma^{-1} (\lambda \bmu + \gamma \bone)
\end{equation}

Plugging this value of $\bw$ into the constraints, we obtain the following system of equations:

\begin{align}
    \lambda\,\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\mu}^T\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{A} + \gamma\,\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\mu}^T\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}_{B} &= \bar r \\
    \lambda\,\underbrace{\mathbf{1}^T\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{B} + \gamma\,\underbrace{\mathbf{1}^T\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}_{C} &= 1
\end{align}
which can be written as the linear system
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ B & C \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ \gamma \end{pmatrix}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix} \bar r \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

Denoting $M = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ B & C \end{pmatrix}$, we have

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        &\begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ \gamma \end{pmatrix} = M^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} \bar r \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \\
        \Rightarrow & \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ \gamma \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{AC - B^2}\begin{pmatrix} C & -B \\ -B & A \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \bar r \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{AC - B^2}\begin{pmatrix} C\bar r - B \\ A - B\bar r \end{pmatrix} \\
        \Rightarrow & \begin{cases} \lambda(\bar r) = \frac{C\bar r - B}{AC - B^2} \\ \gamma(\bar r) = \frac{A - B\bar r}{AC - B^2} \end{cases}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\vspace{1em}

Given $\lambda(\bar r)$ and $\gamma(\bar r)$ from equation (11), the optimal weights follow directly by substituting back into equation (7):

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        & \mathbf{w}(\bar r) = \Sigma^{-1}\big(\lambda(\bar r)\,\boldsymbol{\mu} + \gamma(\bar r)\,\mathbf{1}\big) \\
        \Rightarrow & \mathbf{w}(\bar r) = \frac{1}{AC - B^2}\Sigma^{-1}\big((C\bar r - B)\,\boldsymbol{\mu} + (A - B\bar r)\,\mathbf{1}\big)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In our implementation, we solved this system of equations using the \texttt{numpy.linalg.solve} function, allowing for a more numerically stable computation compared to explicitly inverting the matrix.
\vspace{2em}
In order to trace the curve of the efficient frontier, we simply sweep the target return $\bar r$ from the minimum to the maximum expected return of the assets, and compute the corresponding optimal weights $\bw(\bar r)$ and the associated portfolio variance $\Var(r_p) = \bw(\bar r)^\top \bSigma \bw(\bar r)$.
